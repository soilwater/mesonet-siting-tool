About the method

Patrignani, A., Mohankumar, N., Redmond, C., Santos, E. A., & Knapp, M. (2020). Optimizing the spatial configuration of mesoscale environmental monitoring networks using a geometric approach. Journal of Atmospheric and Oceanic Technology, 37(5), 943–956. doi:10.1175/JTECH-D-19-0167.1

How it works

  1. A Voronoi diagram splits the region among the existing stations. Its vertices, and the points where its edges cross the boundary, are the candidate centers of unmonitored areas.
  2. Around each candidate, the largest circle with no station inside is drawn. Its radius is the distance to the nearest station.
  3. Each circle is clipped to the boundary. The one with the most area inside the region is the largest empty area, and the new station goes at its centroid.
  4. The new station is added to the network and the process repeats, giving a build order for as many stations as needed.

Because circles are clipped, sites on the border count only the area inside the region. With no stations, the first site is the point farthest from the boundary. Distances and areas are computed on an equal-area projection centered on the region, so results are valid anywhere on Earth.

Why not simple buffers?

A common GIS approach draws a fixed buffer around each station and places the next one in the largest uncovered area. In tests on 300 synthetic networks worldwide, the largest-empty-area method left smaller gaps than 25-km buffers in 88% of cases when adding to an existing network, and in 79–100% of cases when building one from scratch. Common pitfalls of the buffer approach:

  • Arbitrary radius. Change the buffer size and the recommended site can move by several km.
  • Merged gaps. With small buffers the uncovered area is one large connected shape, and its centroid sits in the middle of the region, so new stations can cluster together instead of filling the real gaps.
  • Slivers. Uncovered areas are often long, thin strips along borders whose centroid lands on the boundary or next to an existing station.
  • Distortion. Buffers and centroids computed in degrees of latitude and longitude are distorted away from the equator.

Here, buffers are only drawn as each station's footprint.
